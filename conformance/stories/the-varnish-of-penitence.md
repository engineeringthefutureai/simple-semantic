# The Varnish of Penitence

In the damp, vaulted cellar of Blackthorn Manor, Julian worked by the amber beam of a single halogen lamp, surrounded by the acrid sting of turpentine, spirit-varnish, and centuries of dust.

His task was straightforward, if monumental: restore the portrait of Lord Malachi Blackthorn, painted in 1742 and left to rot under two hundred years of coal soot, nicotine, and yellowed mastic varnish. The manor’s modern heirs wanted the portrait cleaned for auction before the roof caved in entirely.

Julian dipped a cotton swab into a mixture of alcohol and mineral spirits, blotting it against a paper towel before gently rolling it across the upper left quadrant of the heavy gilded frame.

The old varnish came off in sloughs of sticky brown resin.

"Foucart's method," Julian muttered to himself, his breath pluming in the chill basement air. "Slow and steady."

Underneath the degraded coating, the original oil pigment bloomed into sudden, startling life. Lord Malachi sat in a high-backed velvet chair, holding a quill over an open ledger. But as Julian cleaned away the grime surrounding the face, he paused.

The historical catalog described Lord Malachi as a stern, aristocratic figure with severe blue eyes and a tight, austere mouth. Yet, as the swab stripped away the discolored resin, the eyes on the canvas were not blue. They were a watery hazel. And the expression wasn't one of austere nobility—the corners of the mouth were pulled down in an agonizing, silent grimace of terror.

Julian frowned, leaning closer. He adjusted his magnifying spectacles.

Perhaps a lower layer of paint—a pentimento—had been covered over by a second artist. He dipped a fresh swab into a stronger solvent, acetone blended with lavender oil, and touched it to the figure’s left hand.

The yellowed varnish dissolved instantly.

Underneath, Lord Malachi was not holding a quill. His fingers were clawing violently at his own throat, leaving dark, crimson gashes in his linen cravat.

Julian gasped, stepping back from the easel. The bottle of acetone tipped over, spilling across the stone workbench with a sharp, sickening chemical reek.

The shadows in the corner of the cellar seemed to lengthen, stretching across the vaulted damp brickwork. The halogen light flickered once, twice, humming at a sickly pitch.

Julian swallowed hard, his throat dry. He told himself it was an optical illusion—a psychological trick produced by toxic solvent fumes in a poorly ventilated room. He reached for his respirator mask, but his fingers hesitated over the handle of his scalpel.

Curiosity—and a rising, cold dread—drove him back to the easel.

He picked up a wide cotton pad soaked in solvent and wiped a broad stroke across the central expanse of the portrait.

The paint beneath shifted before his eyes.

The face in the frame was no longer Lord Malachi’s. As the chemical cleared away the decades, the features reformed, softening and reshaping into a face Julian knew with agonizing intimacy: Thomas.

His younger brother.

Thomas, whose artist portfolio Julian had stolen twenty years ago to secure his own fellowship in Florence. Thomas, who had died alone in a cold studio three years later, broke, bitter, and forgotten, while Julian built a lucrative career on borrowed talent and unspoken lies.

"No," Julian whispered, staggering back against the workbench. "That's impossible. It's an old canvas. It's just a portrait..."

On the canvas, Thomas’s eyes opened wide. They were not painted in static oil; the wet pigments glistened, reflecting the harsh glare of the halogen lamp. A thick, dark teardrop of linseed oil and lampblack welled up in the corner of Thomas’s eye and began to trickle down the linen surface.

The mouth on the painting parted, breaking through the dry, craquelure network of cracks.

*Why did you take my name, Julian?*

The voice did not come from the room. It echoed inside Julian’s skull, vibrating against his teeth like the strike of a tuning fork.

"I gave you money\!" Julian cried out to the empty basement, his hands shaking violently as he grabbed a bottle of pure alcohol to splash over the canvas, intending to destroy the evidence, to melt the paint into a ruined smear of sludge. "I sent you what I could\!"

*You took the light,* the painting whispered back.

The solvent hit the canvas. But instead of dissolving the image, the liquid pooled across the surface like clear glass. The underlying layers of paint surged upward, bleeding through the canvas as if pushed from behind by living hands.

The frame groaned. The canvas stretched outward from its wooden stretcher bars, bulging two inches into the room, pressing against the air like a drowning man pushing against a sheet of ice.

Julian stumbled backward, tripping over his supply crate and crashing to the cold flagstones.

From inside the painting, a hand covered in tacky, wet sienna oil reached out into the dim room, its fingers curling into the air, reaching for Julian’s throat.

The halogen lamp exploded in a sharp shower of sparks, plunging the cellar into absolute, suffocating darkness.

And in the silence that followed, the wet, sliding sound of canvas tearing open echoed right above him.
