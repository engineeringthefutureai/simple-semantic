# The Reverse Escapement

In a city where everything valuable was logged on secure cloud registries, Silas Vance was the guy you hired when you lost something real.

He didn't track missing spouses or run background checks on corporate vice presidents. Silas found brass fountain pens dropped down subway grates, leather-bound diaries left on midnight trams, and grandfather clocks lost in probate battles. In the era of digital tracking, analog items were ghosts—and Silas knew how to read their trails.

The rain was beating a steady, syncopated rhythm against his second-story window when Evelyn Cross walked into his office. She wore a tailored velvet trench coat that smelled of ozone and expensive perfume, her fingers nervously twisting a plain gold ring.

"It was stolen from my grandfather’s safe three hours ago," she said, setting a black velvet box on his desk. Inside was an empty cushion shaped to hold a heavy pocket watch. "It’s a brass-cased mechanical timepiece with three second hands and a counter-rotating escapement. He called it the *Chronos-Vane*."

Silas raised an eyebrow. "A pocket watch? I can trace the pawn networks in the Ironworks district. Give me twelve hours."

"You don't understand, Mr. Vance," Evelyn said, leaning across the desk, her voice dropping to a tense whisper. "It doesn't tell the time of the room you're standing in. When you wind the crown counter-clockwise, the internal prism projects an image through the front crystal. It displays a crime."

"A crime?" Silas chuckled softly, leaning back. "Like a film reel?"

"No," she said, her eyes dead serious. "It displays a crime that hasn't happened yet. My grandfather was an engineer for the Municipal Transit System. He built the watch after discovering that the city’s criminal syndicates used hyper-precise, deterministic timetables to coordinate hit jobs across the rail network. The watch calculates those predictive algorithmic patterns and turns them into a visual projection."

Silas stopped smiling. "And who took it?"

"A local enforcer named Viktor 'The Needle' Vane," she said. "He doesn't know what it does yet. He thinks it's just heavy gold. If he figures out how to wind it backward..."

Silas took the job.

Two hours later, Silas was navigating the damp, steam-filled alleys beneath the elevated mag-lev tracks in the Ironworks district. Viktor operated out of a backroom behind a discarded electronics depot. Silas didn't use a gun; he used a forged police seizure warrant and a purse containing three hundred credits—enough to convince Viktor's nervous guard that handing over an unlisted trinket was better than a precinct raid.

By 11:00 PM, Silas was back in his dimly lit office, the heavy brass pocket watch resting in his palm. It was surprisingly heavy, cold to the touch, and carved with intricate geometric dials.

Curiosity, as always, pulled at his collar.

Silas grasped the knurled brass crown and slowly turned it counter-clockwise. *Click. Click. Click.*

The watch face began to hum with a low, harmonic vibration. The glass lens over the dial glowed with a pale blue light, refracting through miniature internal lenses to project a sharp, flickering image onto the plaster wall of his office.

The projection showed a dark alleyway—the exact alleyway beneath his own office window. Rain lashed against the damp bricks. A figure in a heavy trench coat stood near the fire escape, waiting.

Seconds later in the projection, a second man approached from the street, carrying a briefcase. The figure in the trench coat pulled a silenced pistol, fired twice, and grabbed the briefcase before melting into the shadows.

Silas stared at the projected image, his blood running cold.

The man who stepped into the alley carrying the briefcase wasn't a stranger. It was Silas himself.

He looked at the small clock on his desk. The timestamp projected on the wall read 00:15 AM.

It was currently 11:42 PM. He had exactly thirty-three minutes before he was scheduled to die in the alley below his own front door.

Silas reached for his coat, his pulse racing. The logical choice was simple: leave the office immediately through the rooftop access, avoid the alley, and break the timeline. If determinism governed the city's syndicates, refusing to play the role was the ultimate weapon.

He grabbed the pocket watch and slid it into his pocket. But as he turned toward the roof door, a detail from the projection flashed in his mind.

In the projected video, the killer didn't just ambush him. The killer stepped out from behind the dumpster *before* Silas arrived, already knowing he would take that specific route down the back stairs.

Silas froze. The watch hadn't predicted his future—it had calculated the hitman's precise protocol. But if the hitman relied entirely on the algorithmic timetable, the hitman's actions were totally predictable too.

At 12:12 AM, Silas walked down the internal stairwell to the ground floor. He didn't step out into the rain. Instead, he opened the narrow basement service hatch that opened *behind* the alley's dumpster.

At 12:14 AM, a figure stepped out from the street, pulling a silenced firearm and positioning himself near the fire escape, just as projected.

Silas reached quietly out from the service hatch, brandishing a heavy iron pipe, and brought it down hard on the hitman's wrist. The gun clattered into the wet gravel. Before the assassin could react, Silas tackled him into the rain, pinning him against the brick wall.

The hitman gasped, his hood slipping back.

It wasn't one of Viktor's men.

It was Evelyn Cross.

Silas caught his breath, staring down at her in disbelief. "You," he whispered.

Evelyn glared up at him, her teeth gritted. "My grandfather didn't build that watch to catch criminals, Vance," she hissed. "He built it to plan perfect crimes. It calculates the only path where a target has a zero-percent chance of escape."

"Then why hire me to find it?" Silas demanded.

"Because the algorithm requires the mark to hold the watch," she spat, struggling against his grip. "The projection forces you into the specific alley at the specific time because you think you're trying to outsmart it. The panic is part of the calculation."

Silas looked down at the brass watch lying in the puddle nearby, its hands ticking steadily backward.

He smiled faintly, reached down, and smashed the glass lens under his heavy boot. The gears ground to a halt, spraying tiny brass cogs across the wet cobblestones.

"Algorithm broken," Silas said softly. "Looks like time just ran out."
