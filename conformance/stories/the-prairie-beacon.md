# The Prairie Beacon

The nearest saltwater was six hundred miles west, but Grandfather Gideon’s lighthouse stood sixty feet tall in the middle of an endless Kansas wheat field.

Clara leaned against the rusted passenger door of her station wagon, squinting up at the white-painted cypress siding. The paint was peeling in long, curled ribbons, revealing gray, weathered timber underneath. At the top, the brass housing of the lantern room glinted in the dry afternoon heat.

A gravel-crunching rumble announced Julian’s arrival. He parked his pickup truck ten feet away, killed the engine, and stepped out without a word. He looked tired. The three years since their mother’s funeral had etched hard lines around his eyes and broadened his shoulders, making him look less like the brother Clara had grown up with and more like a stranger wearing his jacket.

"You brought the keys?" Julian asked, his voice low and dry.

"Lawyer said they were under the brass pelican on the porch," Clara said, keeping her tone strictly operational.

They hadn't spoken since the estate dispute three summers ago—a silent, grinding war of bruised egos and unreturned phone calls that had left both of them stranded on opposite sides of a quiet divide. Now, the probate court required them to clear out Gideon’s "folly" before the land could be auctioned off to the neighboring corn conglomerate.

Gideon had spent thirty years building the structure. A former naval machinist, he had insisted that the Great Plains were simply an ocean that had lost its water. His ultimate project—a planned "Land-Yacht and Prairie Navigation Museum"—had never attracted a single paying tourist, leaving behind only three stories of nautical clutter anchored in six hundred acres of golden grain.

Julian unlocked the heavy oak door. The interior smelled of old cedar, dry rot, engine grease, and dried lavender.

"Where do we even start?" Julian muttered, staring at the ground floor.

It was packed to the exposed rafters with Gideon’s hand-built contraptions. Giant wooden wheels with rubber tread, brass sextants modified with spirit levels, and stack upon stack of yellowed blueprints for wind-powered prairie galleons.

"Ground floor is junk, middle floor is tools, top floor is... whatever he was working on when he died," Clara said, consulting the attorney's inventory sheet. "We tag what’s worth keeping for the estate sale and haul the rest to the dump."

They worked in relative silence for three hours. The physical labor was heavy, but the air between them was heavier. Every shared box brought back ghosts. When Julian lifted a crate of copper lantern lenses, Clara instinctively reached out to steady it, their fingers brushing briefly. Both pulled back as if burned.

"He was crazy, you know," Julian said suddenly, wiping sweat from his forehead with the back of his forearm as he stacked a pile of rusted steering wheels. "Spending his pension on teak planks and brass screws while the farmhouse roof was falling in. Mom spent fifteen years trying to get him to come down from here."

"He wasn't crazy," Clara said quietly, holding a bound journal filled with Gideon's precise, copperplate handwriting. "He was just lonely after Grandma died. He said the wind in the wheat sounded like the surf off Cape Hatteras."

"Sounding like surf doesn't pay a mortgage, Clara," Julian snapped, his voice dropping into the familiar edge that had broken their family apart. "Some people live in reality. Some people build forty-foot wooden towers in a field and pretend they're at sea."

"And some people run away to Denver the second things get hard\!" Clara retorted, her chest tightening.

Julian went quiet. He dropped a heavy brass pulley onto the floorboards with a dull *thud*. He didn't argue back. He just turned and began hauling a stack of timber down the narrow spiral staircase.

By seven in the evening, the sun had begun its slow descent toward the horizon, painting the sky in deep shades of violet and crushed orange. Clara carried the last stack of inventory tags up to the lantern room at the top of the tower.

The room was circular, surrounded by heavy glass panes. In the center sat Gideon's masterpiece: a massive glass Fresnel lens mounted on a brass swivel, powered by a bicycle-chain drive mechanism.

She heard Julian's heavy footsteps ascending the iron spiral stairs behind her. He paused in the doorway, holding two lukewarm cans of soda he’d found in the cooler. He extended one toward her.

Clara took it. "Thanks."

Julian walked over to the glass wall, looking out over the expanse.

From sixty feet in the air, the Kansas prairie didn't look like dirt and weeds. The evening breeze rippled through the miles of ripe wheat, creating massive, fluid swells of green and gold that rolled toward the setting sun in endless, rhythmic waves. The horizon stretched out flat and unbounded, so wide it gives you the dizzying sensation of standing on the bow of a ship floating in an ocean of light.

"Look at that," Julian whispered.

Clara looked. In the middle of the central pane, Gideon had scratched a tiny note into the brass frame with a pocketknife:

*For Clara and Julian. When the grass is too tall, come up where you can see the tide.*

Julian traced the etched letters with his thumb. His throat hitched slightly, a small, barely audible sound.

"He knew we’d end up up here," Julian said, his eyes fixed on the horizon. "Clearing out his mess."

"He knew we'd have to look at each other eventually," Clara said gently.

Julian took a slow breath, leaning his forehead against the cool glass. "I shouldn't have left after Mom died, Clara. I was overwhelmed. I didn't know how to fix any of it, so I just... left you with the farm."

The confession hung in the warm, dry air of the lantern room, soft and quiet against the rustle of the wind outside.

Clara stepped beside him, looking out at the golden waves rolling across the valley. "It was heavy, Jules. It really was. But you're here now."

Julian nodded slowly. He reached down to the bicycle-chain mechanism of the Fresnel lens, grabbed the leather-wrapped crank handle, and gave it a gentle push.

The great glass lens began to rotate smoothly on its brass bearings. As the setting sun hit the multifaceted glass, broad beams of amber light swept across the prairie ceiling, illuminating the dark miles of wheat like a beacon guiding lost ships home.

They sat down on the wooden bench together in the glowing light, watching the tide go out.
